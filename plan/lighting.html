Lighting

The lights are the art: a lit wall on every pod, a mirror skirt under each floor, and the roof rim you see from across the playa — all addressable, all on from sunset to sunrise. This page sets the power budget: the assumptions first, then how many LEDs each zone gets and how bright they actually run, calibrated against what the current car draws tonight rather than what a data sheet says it could.

Where this lands. The plan pages have carried lighting at 0.3–1 kW (2.4–8 kWh a night). Calibrated against the current car — ~3,300 LEDs through most of a night on one 100 Ah 12 V LiFePO4 — the same style of art on the new car is ~200–300 W: 2–3 kWh a night, about an hour of generator, under 10 % of the pack. The lever that matters is the number of LEDs, not the brightness slider, because a dark addressable LED still draws power.

What has been decided

  • Zones and modules. Every lit element is a module with one connector: twelve light boxes per pod (four rows at 60/m, ~530 LEDs a pod, from the pods page), a strip washing the mirror skirt under each pod floor, the 3″ roof-rim fascia lit from inside (roof page), plus the stairs, the driver's console and the ottomans. Controller, power distribution and harness terminals live in each pod's end consoles; every zone has its own 12 V rail off the sled through a DC-DC converter (electrical page). decided
  • The budget. Design to ~2.2 kWh a night (2.5 with the 12 V WS2815 option): about 4,700 LEDs running at a 10 % average power fraction, ~200 W average, ~1.6 kW if everything went full white (it never does; see the limiter below). That is the current car's real consumption scaled up ~40 % for ~40 % more LEDs — the same ~42 mW per LED — and it is ~9 % of the usable pack, or an hour of generator. direction
  • Calibrate, don't assume. The budget's two soft numbers — the art's average power fraction and the LEDs' standing draw — are both measurable on the current car this year with the SmartShunt already on the BOM and a five-minute bench test. They get measured before the LED count is frozen. decided
  • A firmware power limiter is part of the design. FastLED's setMaxPowerInVoltsAndMilliamps() caps each controller's output whatever a pattern asks for; set it at ~30 % of the zone's full-white figure, fuse each zone a little above the cap, and size the wiring to the fuse rather than to full white. direction
  • Controller. The current car's custom Teensy 3.2 / FastLED board (QueenFX, 16 parallel outputs at ~273 LEDs each) is the starting point; whether the new car gets one board per pod in the console or one central board is open. direction
  • LED type. WS2812B (5 V) is what the pod modules were drawn with; WS2815 (12 V) is what the current car runs — half the current for the same light and a backup data line so one dead pixel does not black out the chain, at the cost of roughly three times the standing draw (~40 W across the car). Decide per zone after the standing-draw measurement. open

Assumptions behind the power budget

The budget is one line of arithmetic per zone: average watts = N × (standing draw + power fraction × 0.3 W) ÷ efficiency, plus the controllers, times the hours lit. Every input is listed here with where it comes from and how to check it. The two that move the answer most are the power fraction (row 5) and the standing draw (row 4); everything else is either known or a choice.

Calibration: the current car

1,280 Wh at 92 % usable is ~1,180 Wh. Over ~8.5 hours that is ~140 W average for 3,306 LEDs: ~42 mW per LED all-in, 3.5 mA at 12 V — about 14 % of the 0.3 W full-white figure, standing draw included. Take off ~10 mW of standing draw and the art itself runs at ~30 mW, a 10 % power fraction. The lantern is a second point in the same code style: 347 WS2812 all lit at 1.5 A and 5 V is 22 mW per LED, 7 %.

That is half of what the models assume. loveseat-build and frame-3d both carry 0.08 W per LED as "typical" (27 % of full white), and the notebook's 0.3–1 kW for the current car's LED count works out at 90–300 mW per LED — the top of that range is every LED at full white, which no pattern ever does. Those figures are two to seven times the measured baseline; the drivetrain review had already corrected the lighting line once, by 2.5×, in the other direction. The sensitivity is simple: if "most of the night" is 9.5 hours the baseline is 37 mW, if it is 8 hours 45 mW, and if two batteries are used in a night 85 mW. The SmartShunt settles it.

The strips are comparable across the two cars: the current car's LEDs sit inside translucent PEX, the new car's inside opal-lidded ply boxes and a 3″ fascia — diffused in both cases, so the fraction transfers without a correction for the diffuser.

What it means for the rest of the car: 2.2 kWh is ~9 % of the 23–26 kWh usable pack, or one hour of the camp generator through the 48 V charger, against three to five hours for the driving. On its own bank, a 200 Ah 12 V LiFePO4 (2.4 kWh usable, ~45 lb) runs the budget for a full night with a little margin; the current 100 Ah battery would run it for about six hours, and the as-drawn count for four.

Spending fewer watts, artistically

The current car's patterns already sit at a 10 % fraction, so the art does not have to change to hit the budget; these are the places the watts hide and the ways to keep them low without making the car dimmer.

  • Count, not brightness. At a 10 % fraction every LED costs 3–12 mW just to be present, so a dark pixel is not free. Halving the rim and skirt density and dropping the underside wash row removes ~2,300 LEDs and ~100 W, and nothing visible from 100 ft changes.
  • Dark between the light. Sparse patterns — comets, breathing, slow gradients with black between them — read as contrast, which the eye takes for brightness. Keeping the fraction of pixels lit low is a better dial than turning the global brightness down, because it keeps the peaks.
  • Saturated palettes. A hue lights one channel; white lights three. A pastel-and-white night is the 20 % case in the table.
  • Big soft surfaces. A large diffused glow at low brightness reads brighter at night than points of light — the pod light boxes and the PEX hoops are already this. Edge-lit light-guide panels take it further: a laser-etched dot gradient on clear acrylic lights a 12 × 9″ module evenly from one 12″ edge, 29 LEDs instead of 44, no scallops, and a thinner module. Worth building one beside a back-lit one; the pods page already wants a first module to check for scalloping.
  • Not everything needs pixels. Where nothing moves — the underside wash, the deck, the stair treads — a warm-white strip on a dimmer does the job at a fraction of the standing draw and none of the data wiring.
  • A late-night mode. 60 % after 2 am, from the clock or a photodiode, saves ~0.25 kWh and nobody notices.
  • The limiter as the safety net. With setMaxPowerInVoltsAndMilliamps() at ~30 % of full white per zone, a runaway pattern or a full-white test cannot flatten the battery or the wiring; the budget is enforced, not hoped for.

  8. Whether lighting gets its own 12 V bank so the lit perimeter survives a drive-pack BMS trip, or the sled's DC-DC with a low-voltage cutoff that spares the lights.

How we got here

Lighting lived on the electrical page as one bullet and one line of the energy budget, carrying the notebook's 0.3–1 kW estimate for the current car's LED count — a figure the drivetrain review had already corrected once. The models carried 0.08 W per LED as typical. On 13 September the current car's actual battery behaviour (one 100 Ah 12 V LiFePO4, most of a night) was used to calibrate the per-LED figure, and the count of what the pages actually draw was added up; the result was this page, the proposed count, and the brief to keep the art sparse and saturated rather than dim. The hoop lighting on the current car (WS2815 in PEX on steel, one outward-facing strip per hoop to conserve battery) is the direct ancestor of the light boxes and the rim; Selina's sketches 3, 5, 7 and 9 are the references for the lit rim and for small lampshades on the pods.
